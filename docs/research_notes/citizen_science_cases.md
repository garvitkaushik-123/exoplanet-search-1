# Citizen-science, student and amateur exoplanet and substellar-companion discoveries in public space-mission data (2012-2026): case notes and operating procedures

Facts below come from web-search snippets of the abstracts, journal and repository pages, and press releases. ## Q1. Case-by-case notes (who, when, data, niche, method, vetting, follow-up, venue, timeline, lesson)

### Takeaway
Every successful amateur or citizen discovery followed one pattern. Humans flagged a signal that automated BLS/TPS-style pipelines handle poorly: a single transit, a long period, a circumbinary planet with variable timing and duration, or an odd shape. Professionals then vetted it (pixel-level checks, centroids, nearby EBs) and arranged follow-up (TFOP or ExoFOP, RVs, imaging, ground photometry), and published with the volunteers as co-authors or acknowledged. Long-period and single-transit planets around bright stars dominate the confirmed TESS-era wins.

### Cited Findings

**Planet Hunters (Kepler, Zooniverse, 2010-) — PH1b / Kepler-64b**
- Volunteers found PH1b in the first six quarters of public Kepler data. It is the first known transiting planet in a quadruple star system. Its transits across the brighter eclipsing star are "detectable by visual inspection every ~137 days", and 7 transits were identified in Q1-Q11. Paper: Schwamb et al., "Planet Hunters: A Transiting Circumbinary Planet in a Quadruple Star System"; Chris Lintott is a co-author. — [arXiv 1210.3612](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.3612)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** The volunteer discoverers were Kian Jek and Robert Gagliano, both co-authors. The paper was published in ApJ 768:127 (2013), and the preprint appeared in October 2012.
- Lesson: circumbinary transits are aperiodic in duration and timing, so periodic-search pipelines miss them. The human eye catches a dip "between" eclipses.

**Planet Hunters — PH2b and long-period candidates (Planet Hunters V, Wang et al. 2013)**
- The paper reports a confirmed planet (PH2b) plus 42 candidates. Most have periods over 100 days, and 20 may lie in their stars' habitable zones. Co-authors include volunteer Kian J. Jek and Chris Lintott. — [arXiv 1301.0644](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.0644); press: [NBC News "Amateur astronomers discover 42 alien planets"](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna50449282)
- PH2b was described as a Jupiter-sized planet in the habitable zone. — [SpaceRef](https://spaceref.com/?p=66120)
- Naming: one later paper calls PH2b "Kepler-86b". This rests on a single source and should be treated as unconfirmed. — [arXiv 1310.5912](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1310.5912.pdf)
- Early history: the first Planet Hunters candidates were announced in September 2011. — [Planet Hunters blog](https://blog.planethunters.org/2011/09/22/first_two_planet_candidates/)

**Exoplanet Explorers (Zooniverse, K2) — K2-138 (2017-2018)**
- K2-138 is a K dwarf (V=12.2) from K2 Campaign 12 with five transiting sub-Neptunes (1.6-3.3 R⊕). Periods are 2.35, 3.56, 5.40, 8.26 and 12.76 d, forming an unbroken chain of near-3:2 resonances. — [Christiansen et al. 2018, AJ 155, 57 (arXiv 1801.03874)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.03874); [Caltech record](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/jk257-3an27)
- The project started in April 2017. Volunteers judged diagnostic plots of pipeline-generated candidate signals (real transit or not). About 14,000 volunteers took part, per the acknowledgments. The paper calls it the first exoplanet discovery by citizen scientists via this project. Published 11 Jan 2018, so detection to publication took about 8-9 months. — [arXiv 1801.03874](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.03874); [Exoplanet Archive PDF](https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/docs/aj_155_2_57.pdf)
- No TTVs were detected at K2 precision; the authors suggested Spitzer or CHEOPS for follow-up. — same
- **[UNVERIFIED]** The candidate was launched live on the ABC Australia "Stargazing Live" TV broadcast; volunteers flagged it within about 48 h. Follow-up included Keck/HIRES spectroscopy and imaging.
- Lesson: a different human role from PH. Here volunteers vetted machine-found candidates (a classification task) rather than searching raw light curves.

**Wolf Cukier (high-school intern, NASA Goddard) — TOI-1338 b (2019-2020)**
- Cukier, a high-school student working with Veselin Kostov, spotted an unexplained transit that did not line up with the binary's eclipses and alerted Kostov. It was the first circumbinary planet from TESS. — [Wikipedia TOI-1338 b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TOI-1338_b)
- The headline says he found it on the 3rd day of his internship. — [NY Photonics](https://newyorkphotonics.org/high-school-student-17-discovers-a-planet-nearly-seven-times-the-size-of-earth-during-the-third-day-of-his-internship-at-nasa/)
- Method context: the Goddard team was visually inspecting TESS eclipsing-binary light curves (Planet Hunters EB flags were also used) for extra transits. **[UNVERIFIED in detail; the involvement of PH volunteer EB lists is from memory.]**
- The planet has P = 95.2 d, R ≈ 6.9 R⊕ and e ≈ 0.09. It made three transits across the primary of about equal depth (0.2%) but different durations. Paper: Kostov et al. 2020, AJ 159, 253, "TOI-1338: TESS' First Transiting Circumbinary Planet". — [sci.news](https://www.sci.news/astronomy/tess-circumbinary-planet-toi-1338b-07989.html); [Oxford Zooniverse pubs](https://www.physics.ox.ac.uk/research/group/zooniverse/publications?page=32)
- Discovery in summer 2019 and announcement on 7 Jan 2020 at AAS 235 (Honolulu): about 6 months to announcement. — [SDSU news](https://www.sdsu.edu/news/2020/01/astronomers-pinpoint-two-new-double-star-planetary-systems); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/pub_releases/2020-01/sdsu-sap010720.php)
- Later RV gave only an upper mass limit (3σ < 12 M⊕) and hinted at a possible second planet. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TOI-1338_b)
- Confirmation of a circumbinary planet relies on photodynamical modelling of transit times and durations against the binary orbit, not on RVs. **[Inference supported by the Kostov abstract, which reports variable durations.]**

**TIC 172900988 (circumbinary, 2021)**
- A transiting circumbinary planet detected in one TESS sector (S21). It transited the primary and then, about 5 days later, the secondary. The binary has P ≈ 19.7 d and e ≈ 0.45. Archival ASAS-SN, Evryscope, KELT and SuperWASP data show apsidal motion. R = 11.25 R⊕; mass 2.65-3.09 MJ; six degenerate period solutions (188.8-204.1 d); V = 10.14. Kostov et al. 2021, AJ 162, 234. — [ADS 2021AJ....162..234K](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....162..234K/abstract); [IAC page](https://www.iac.es/en/science-and-technology/publications/tic-172900988-transiting-circumbinary-planet-detected-one-sector-tess-data)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Whether the planet was first flagged by Planet Hunters TESS volunteers or by the Kostov team's own EB inspection. I could not confirm this. The search did not tie it to PHT.

**Planet Hunters TESS (PHT; Nora Eisner, Oxford/Zooniverse, launched 2018-12)**
- *TOI-813 b (PHT I, Eisner et al. 2020, MNRAS)*: the first PHT planet (TIC 55525572 b). It is a Saturn-sized planet (6.71 ± 0.38 R⊕, P ≈ 83.9 d) around a bright (V = 10.3) subgiant, found by citizen scientists and "validated" rather than confirmed by RV. — [arXiv 1909.09094 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1909.09094); [ANU portal](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/planet-hunters-tess-i-toi-813-a-subgiant-hosting-a-transiting-sat/); [Exoplanet Archive](https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/overview/TOI-813). Preprint Sept 2019, about 9 months after project launch.
  - Note: the brief's pairing "TIC 172900988" with PHT is a different system (above). TOI-813 = TIC 55525572.
- *PHT II catalogue (Eisner et al. 2021, MNRAS 501, 4669)*: more than 22,000 volunteers inspected TESS sectors 1-26 (2-min cadence). PHT recovered 85% of TOIs with R > 4 R⊕ and 51% with 3-4 R⊕. It presented the 90 most promising new candidates not found by other teams; **73 of the 90 show only a single transit**. — [MNRAS](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/501/4/4669/6027708); [IAC summary](https://iac.es/en/science-and-technology/publications/planet-hunters-tess-ii-findings-first-two-years-tess)
- PHT vetting pipeline: (1) multiple volunteers mark transits in the Zooniverse interface; (2) volunteer and team visual vetting, mainly with the open-source **LATTE** tool, with models and reports from LATTE and **pyaneti**; (3) candidates that pass all rounds are uploaded to **ExoFOP-TESS as community TOIs (CTOIs)**; (4) a third stage of ground-based photometry, reconnaissance spectroscopy and speckle imaging. — [search summary of PHT II and related papers via OUP](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/501/4/4669/6027708); [TOI catalog paper, Guerrero et al. 2021 ApJS](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4365/abefe1)
- Candidates are manually checked by multiple project members and must pass tests before Eisner uploads them to ExoFOP. This is a weaker source. — [Wikipedia Planet Hunters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet_Hunters)
- The TOI catalog paper notes PHT CTOIs being promoted to TOIs. — [Guerrero et al. 2021](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4365/abefe1)
- By Dec 2021 PHT volunteers had classified all 2-min data up to sector 34 and found more than 115 previously unknown planet candidates. — [IAS seminar abstract](https://www.ias.edu/node/238351)
- *HD 152843 (PHT III, Eisner et al. 2021, MNRAS 505, 1827)*: three transits from two planets in Sector 25 around a V = 8.85 G dwarf, found by citizen scientists. RVs give a tentative mass of 11.56 M⊕ for b and an upper limit of 27.5 M⊕ for c. — [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/planet-hunters-tess-iii-two-transiting-planets-around-the-bright-/); [PHT blog](https://blog.planethunters.org/2021/06/10/5903/)
- *PHT IV*: a compact hierarchical triple star (TIC 470710327) is mentioned in Eisner's Dec 2021 seminar abstract. — [IAS](https://www.ias.edu/node/238351). **[UNVERIFIED]** PHT IV was published in MNRAS in 2022.
- *TOI-4633 c "Percival" (PHT V, Eisner et al. 2024, AJ 167, 241)*: a mini-Neptune (3.2 R⊕), P ≈ 271.9 d, in the optimistic habitable zone of a V = 9.0 G dwarf in a binary (companion P ≈ 230 yr, e ≈ 0.9). RVs reveal a non-transiting candidate at 34.15 d. Eisner: "While our algorithms struggle to identify these longer-period planets, the citizen scientists don't." — [Caltech record](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/6c9bn-gjp77); [IOP AJ](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-3881/ad1d5c); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2024-04-citizen-scientists-exoplanet-binary-star.pdf); [astrobiology.com](https://astrobiology.com/2024/05/02/planet-hunters-tess-v-a-planetary-system-around-a-binary-star-including-a-mini-neptune-in-the-habitable-zone/)
  - Correction to the brief: Percival was announced in **2024**, not 2022, and it is a mini-Neptune, not "Jupiter-like". The **Jan 2022 "Citizen Scientists Spot Jupiter-like Planet"** story is **TOI-2180 b**, found by Tom Jacobs of the Visual Survey Group (below). — [SolarDaily / NASA reprint](https://www.solardaily.com/reports/Citizen_Scientists_Spot_Jupiter_like_Planet_in_NASA_TESS_Data_999.html); [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/science/citizen-scientists-find-jupiter-like-planet-nasa-says). **[UNVERIFIED]** That NPR covered Percival; NPR may instead have covered TOI-2180 b.
- A 2026 preprint applies LATTE to "ambiguous planetary candidates" in TESS and reports 9 potential candidates, showing LATTE in use outside PHT. — [arXiv 2607.08132](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.08132)

**Planet Hunters NGTS (launched 19 Oct 2021)**
- The first PH project on ground-based survey data (NGTS, Paranal). — [PH blog](https://blog.planethunters.org/2021/10/19/welcome-to-a-new-planet-hunters)
- The first paper (AJ 167, 2024; DOI 10.3847/1538-3881/ad32c8) covers 138,198 light curves from NGTS Public Data Releases 1-2, reviewed by more than 8,000 volunteers. Its top 5 candidates are short-period giants. A companion paper (O'Brien et al. 2024) reports PH-NGTS recovers 74% of NGTS-confirmed planets and 63% of TOIs seen by NGTS. — [search summary; Wikipedia Planet Hunters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet_Hunters); [UCL record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/cgi/export/eprint/10191906/BibTeX/discovery-eprint-10191906.bib)
- NGTS-EB-8 is a double-lined M+M eclipsing binary found by volunteers, who are listed as co-authors (2025). — [arXiv 2510.11575](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.11575)

**Visual Survey Group (VSG): Tom Jacobs, Daryll LaCourse, Mark Omohundro, Hans Martin Schwengeler, et al.**
- A pro-am collaboration searching Kepler, K2 and TESS by eye. It has surveyed nearly 10 million light curves and authored 69 peer-reviewed papers, mainly on exoplanets and multistellar systems. Members use **LcTools** (written by Alan R. Schmitt), which gave the group its name. History paper: Kristiansen, Rappaport, Vanderburg, Jacobs, LaCourse et al. 2022 (12 authors), "The Visual Survey Group: A Decade of Hunting Exoplanets and Unusual Stellar Events with Space-Based Telescopes". — [arXiv 2205.07832](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.07832); [NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230003432)
- **TOI-2180 b (2022)**: Tom Jacobs (a former US naval officer) spotted a single dimming of less than 0.5% lasting about 24 h in TESS data from 1 Feb 2020. The VSG alerted professional collaborators Paul Dalba (UC Riverside) and Diana Dragomir (UNM). Dalba used the Automated Planet Finder (Lick) RVs and organised a ground-based transit campaign across 14 sites. That campaign caught no clear second transit but constrained the period. The planet is 2.8 MJ, P ≈ 260.8 ± 0.6 d, e ≈ 0.368, around a slightly evolved G5 star. Paper: Dalba et al. 2022, "The TESS-Keck Survey VIII", AJ 163, 61; Jacobs is a co-author. — [arXiv 2201.04146](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/2201.04146); [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/the-tess-keck-survey-viii-confirmation-of-a-transiting-giant-plan/); [NASA/SolarDaily](https://www.solardaily.com/reports/Citizen_Scientists_Spot_Jupiter_like_Planet_in_NASA_TESS_Data_999.html)
  - Timeline: transit in Feb 2020, then about 2 years to the AJ paper (preprint Jan 2022). A follow-up RNAAS paper refined the ephemeris after TESS caught a 2nd transit in Sector 48, with Dalba, Jacobs and other citizen co-authors. — [RNAAS 10.3847/2515-5172/ac64fd](https://zendy.io/title/10.3847/2515-5172/ac64fd)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Other VSG-linked results include Kepler/K2 single-transit papers (Uehara et al. 2016), KIC 8462852 ("Boyajian's star"; Planet Hunters volunteers incl. LaCourse), the K2 "disintegrating" planet and white-dwarf debris (WD 1145+017-related), and many TESS triple and quadruple star systems with Rappaport and Kostov.

**Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 (WISE/NEOWISE, launched Feb 2017)**
- Volunteers inspect flip-book animations of unWISE coadds for moving objects and submit a "Think You've Got One" form; project scientists check the motion. The first brown dwarf, WISEA J110125.95+540052.8, was flagged six days after launch and confirmed with IRTF 3 m spectroscopy. Four citizen scientists co-authored Kuchner et al. 2017, ApJL 841, L19. — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017ApJ...841L..19K/abstract); [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/goddard/nasa-funded-citizen-science-project-discovers-new-brown-dwarf/); [AAS Nova](https://aasnova.org/2017/05/30/citizen-scientists-in-search-of-failed-stars/)
- Launch to publication took about 3 months. NASA reported about 117 more brown-dwarf candidates soon after. The long-term target is Y dwarfs. Partners: NASA, UC Berkeley, AMNH, ASU, STScI, Zooniverse. — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/goddard/nasa-funded-citizen-science-project-discovers-new-brown-dwarf/); [NOIRLab](https://noirlab.edu/public/products/citizenscienceprograms/citizenscienceprogram003)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** Later BYW results: hundreds of new cold brown dwarfs (Meisner et al. 2020; Kirkpatrick et al. 2021); the extreme-T subdwarf "The Accident" (2020); white dwarf + brown dwarf wide pairs; and the "Backyard Worlds: Cool Neighbors" spin-off.

**Ground-based amateur follow-up networks**
- **Unistellar** (eVscope, SETI Institute). Citizens gathered data on TOI-2010 b over several years to help confirm it and narrow its period to about 141 d. Mann et al. (AJ) give P = 141.83403 d for a Jovian planet with a single TESS transit. — [Unistellar](https://www.unistellar.com/?p=251247); [DOAJ AJ Jan 2023](https://doaj.org/article/edbefb3d1cb1453aa8a4ed567f354805)
- Unistellar also observed Kepler-167 e (P = 1,071 d) in a 32-h campaign in Nov 2021 with 31 citizen astronomers. — [Unistellar](https://www.unistellar.com/?p=251247)
- Peluso et al. 2023 (PASP 135, 015001): the Unistellar Exoplanet Campaign reports 281 transit detections; citizens routinely measure 1% depths. — [USQ PDF](https://research.usq.edu.au/download/6b85401bb00c5cb1f8b2e38fa4806b50920273e1504b8dc49e50c61f955925e6/2000373/O%E2%80%99Conner_Peluso_2023_PASP_135_015001.pdf)
- **ExoClock** (Ariel ephemeris project). Project IV is a homogeneous catalogue of 620 ephemerides from about 30,000 measurements (amateur networks, literature, Kepler/K2/TESS). It includes 277 TESS planets; 45% needed updates, uncertainty improved by about 10x, and new TTV systems were flagged (ApJS). — [arXiv 2511.14407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.14407); [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/184907)
- **Exoplanet Watch** (NASA/JPL). A WASP-77A b study combined 34 citizen transits from Exoplanet Watch, ExoClock and ETD, including telescopes as small as a 4.5-inch Unistellar. It argues citizen networks refresh ephemerides cheaply (citing Zellem et al. 2020). — [arXiv 2405.19615](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.19615); [Kuchner/Zellem NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011067/downloads/Kuchner_Utilizing%20Small%20Telescopes%20Operated%20by%20Citizen.pdf). **[UNVERIFIED]** Exoplanet Watch provides the EXOTIC reduction software.
- **TFOP SG1 / KELT Follow-Up Network**: **[UNVERIFIED]** SG1 (seeing-limited photometry, coordinated by Karen Collins, using AstroImageJ and the TESS Transit Finder) includes many amateur observatories that rule out nearby eclipsing binaries (NEBs) in TOI apertures. It grew out of the KELT Follow-Up Network. I retrieved no source for this in this session.

### Inferences
- The confirmed citizen wins in TESS (TOI-813 b, HD 152843 b/c, TOI-2180 b, TOI-4633 c, TOI-1338 b) are almost all long-period (over 80 d), single or few transit, circumbinary, or bright-star (V < 10.5) systems. For a small team, the best niche is single transits on bright stars where RVs (APF, HARPS-N, etc.) can follow up.
- The hand-off pattern is consistent. Volunteers flag the signal; a professional "sponsor" (Eisner, Kostov, Dalba, Vanderburg/Rappaport, Christiansen, Kuchner) runs pixel-level vetting, posts a CTOI or uses existing TOI status, and secures RV, imaging or photometry. Volunteers are co-authors (VSG, BYW, PH1, NGTS-EB-8) or acknowledged en masse (Exoplanet Explorers, PHT).
- Detection to publication took about 3 months (BYW brown dwarf, with fast IRTF spectroscopy) to about 6-9 months (K2-138, TOI-1338, TOI-813) for well-placed targets. It took about 2+ years for single-transit giants needing RV period recovery (TOI-2180 b) or HZ mini-Neptunes (TOI-4633 c).

## Q2. Why did humans find what pipelines missed?

### Takeaway
Pipelines such as SPOC/TPS and QLP require two or more periodic transits and assume strict periodicity. Humans excel at single transits, long periods, circumbinary transits with variable timing and duration, odd shapes, and events next to data gaps or systematics.

### Cited Findings
- 73 of PHT's 90 new candidates are single-transit events. The authors stress PHT's sensitivity to single transits and therefore long periods, which pipelines tend to miss. — [Eisner et al. 2021 MNRAS](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/501/4/4669/6027708)
- "While our algorithms struggle to identify these longer-period planets, the citizen scientists don't." (Eisner, on TOI-4633 c) — [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2024-04-citizen-scientists-exoplanet-binary-star.pdf)
- PH1b transits recur about every 137 d and were seen by visual inspection; the circumbinary geometry gives non-periodic transits. — [arXiv 1210.3612](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.3612)
- TOI-1338 b transits had equal depths but different durations. TIC 172900988 b transited both stars 5 days apart within one sector. Both patterns break periodic-search assumptions. — [sci.news](https://www.sci.news/astronomy/tess-circumbinary-planet-toi-1338b-07989.html); [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....162..234K/abstract)
- TOI-2180 b showed only a single transit in TESS Cycle 2. — [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/the-tess-keck-survey-viii-confirmation-of-a-transiting-giant-plan/)
- Counterpoint: PHT recovers only 51% of 3-4 R⊕ TOIs, so humans are weaker than pipelines for small, shallow, multi-transit signals. — [MNRAS](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/501/4/4669/6027708)

### Inferences
- A small team should not compete with SPOC/QLP on short-period periodic signals. It should target what the pipelines skip:
  - single or duo transits (including duotransits across TESS cycles years apart)
  - transits in eclipsing-binary light curves
  - FFI-only stars without 2-min data
  - events near momentum dumps or orbit gaps

- No quantitative per-pipeline miss rate for single transits was retrieved.

## Q3. Tools volunteers used

### Takeaway
LcTools (VSG) and the Zooniverse interface (PH, PHT, EE, BYW) for detection; LATTE (Eisner) plus pyaneti for vetting and modelling; and for ground follow-up, AstroImageJ, EXOTIC and the Unistellar app **[last three partly UNVERIFIED]**.

### Cited Findings
- LcTools by Alan R. Schmitt is VSG's visual-inspection software. — [arXiv 2205.07832](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.07832)
- LATTE (Lightcurve Analysis Tool for Transiting Exoplanets) and pyaneti were used for PHT vetting and modelling. — [OUP PHT II](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/501/4/4669/6027708); [Oxford pubs listing "LATTE"](https://www.physics.ox.ac.uk/research/group/zooniverse/publications?page=26)
- Backyard Worlds uses flip-book animations of unWISE coadds. — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/goddard/nasa-funded-citizen-science-project-discovers-new-brown-dwarf/)
- Exoplanet Explorers had volunteers rate diagnostic plots of candidate transits. — [arXiv 1801.03874](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.03874)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** LATTE (Eisner et al. 2020, JOSS) checks the following:
  - momentum dumps and background flux
  - centroid shifts
  - aperture-size dependence
  - nearby-star light curves
  - pixel-level per-pixel light curves
  - odd/even depths
  - TPF images with Gaia sources
  - optional BLS and eleanor FFI extraction

  Lightkurve and eleanor are widely used for FFI light curves.

## Q4. Professional vetting before announcement, and the hand-off and credit

### Takeaway
Vetting is staged: volunteer consensus, then expert visual and pixel-level vetting (LATTE), then CTOI on ExoFOP, then TFOP ground photometry, reconnaissance spectroscopy and speckle imaging, then RV mass or statistical validation. Credit ranges from full co-authorship (VSG, BYW, PH1, NGTS-EB-8, Unistellar TOI-6883) to collective acknowledgment (thousands of volunteers).

### Cited Findings
- PHT has three stages: volunteer classification; LATTE and pyaneti vetting by multiple volunteers and team members; and ground follow-up (photometry, recon spectroscopy, speckle). Candidates passing all rounds are uploaded to ExoFOP as CTOIs. — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/501/4/4669/6027708); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet_Hunters)
- The TESS Science Office group vetting assigns follow-up priorities to the five TFOP subgroups. PHT CTOIs were promoted to TOIs. — [Guerrero et al. 2021 ApJS](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4365/abefe1)
- TOI-813 b was statistically validated. HD 152843 got only tentative RV masses. TOI-4633 c got RVs plus high-resolution imaging, which revealed a stellar companion. — [ar5iv 1909.09094](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1909.09094); [Princeton](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/planet-hunters-tess-iii-two-transiting-planets-around-the-bright-/); [Caltech](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/6c9bn-gjp77)
- VSG hand-off: the VSG alerts professional collaborators (Dalba, Dragomir), who then run RVs (APF) and a 14-site photometry campaign. Jacobs is a co-author. — [NASA/SolarDaily](https://www.solardaily.com/reports/Citizen_Scientists_Spot_Jupiter_like_Planet_in_NASA_TESS_Data_999.html); [arXiv 2201.04146](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/2201.04146)
- BYW: volunteers submit a form, scientists verify motion, then IRTF spectroscopy; 4 volunteers became co-authors. — [AAS Nova](https://aasnova.org/2017/05/30/citizen-scientists-in-search-of-failed-stars/)
- Circumbinary cases were confirmed via photodynamics plus archival survey data (ASAS-SN, KELT, SuperWASP, Evryscope). — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AJ....162..234K/abstract)

### Inferences (transferable lessons for a small TESS team)
1. Pick a niche pipelines under-serve: single or duo transits on bright (V < 11) FGK stars, transits in EB light curves, and FFI-only targets.
2. Run LATTE-style checks yourself before contacting anyone: centroids, nearby Gaia sources, NEB contamination, odd/even, background, momentum dumps.
3. Partner early with a professional sponsor who can submit or support a CTOI and access RVs. Every confirmed case had one.
4. Use the ExoFOP CTOI mechanism so TFOP SG1 (amateur and pro photometry), SG2 (recon spectroscopy) and SG3 (imaging) can engage.
5. Expect about 6 months to 2+ years. Long-period single transits need RV period recovery or a later TESS sector (as with TOI-2180 b in S48).
6. Use citizen photometry networks (Unistellar, ExoClock, Exoplanet Watch) to recover predicted transits of long-period candidates (as with TOI-2010 b and Kepler-167 e).
